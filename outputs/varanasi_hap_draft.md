## ⚠ Important Notice — Use With Caution

This document was generated automatically from satellite-derived data. Its
knowledge of Varanasi is limited to what satellites can measure: surface
temperature, land cover, building density, nighttime lights and gridded
population. **It does not know what any specific parcel of land is or means
to the people who use it.** In particular, this Plan cannot distinguish a
school, hospital, playground, market, ghat, cremation ground, place of
worship, heritage precinct or community gathering space from any other built
or open area.

Accordingly:

- **No figure or priority in this Plan authorises any physical action.**
  Every proposed intervention — roof coating, tree planting, or any other —
  must be preceded by a ground survey and consultation with the ward's
  residents and the relevant custodians of the land.
- **Nothing in this Plan should be read as recommending demolition,
  clearance, or repurposing of any existing structure or open ground.** A
  ward flagged "High risk" or "CRITICAL" describes measured heat conditions,
  not a licence to displace what is already there.
- Classifications (High/Medium/Low) are **relative to Varanasi's own
  distribution** — a "Low" ward is not heat-safe.
- Population figures are modelled (WorldPop 2020) and must be reconciled with
  current municipal records before resource allocation.
- Sections marked **[BLANK]** are not optional: the Plan is incomplete and
  not actionable until qualified local officials complete them.

**Data-quality note specific to Varanasi:** three wards in the source ward
shapefile carry no ward identifier or name. They appear in this Plan's tables
as "Ward 0" with identical statistics, because their records cannot be told
apart. Their individual figures are unreliable until the municipal GIS ward
boundary file is corrected; they are flagged wherever they appear.

This draft is a decision-support starting point for the municipal planning
authority — not a substitute for its judgement.

## 1. Executive Summary

Varanasi experiences substantial summer heat exposure across its urban core.
Over the 89 municipal wards analysed (69.0 km²), the mean daytime land
surface temperature during the summer season was 43.1 °C, with the hottest
tenth of the city's surface exceeding 44.6 °C and the hottest one percent
exceeding 45.5 °C. Of the city's 69.0 km², 65.7 km² — nearly the entire
municipal area — exceeded 40 °C, and 2.5 km² exceeded 45 °C.

Under the Composite Heat Risk Index, which follows the IPCC AR6 framework
(Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability), 27 wards are classified High
risk, 18 Medium and 44 Low. An estimated 286,958 residents (WorldPop 2020)
live in the High-risk wards. The five highest-risk wards are Jalalipur (Risk
Index 3.53), Jamalugheenpur (3.39), Jolha (3.37), Kazisadullapura (3.34) and
Nawapura (3.34); the full ranking of all 89 wards is provided in Section 6.

Two data-grounded intervention programmes are recommended for immediate
appraisal (Section 7):

- **Cool roofs.** In the 27 wards classified High priority for cool-roof
  intervention, 1.62 km² of dark roof surface (albedo below 0.20) requires
  reflective treatment, at an estimated coating cost of ₹3,074 lakh
  (₹150-230/m², midpoint ₹190/m²).
- **Urban greening.** In the 24 wards classified High priority for tree
  planting, the tree-canopy deficit against the 20% national planning target
  totals 736 ha, requiring an estimated 148,987 saplings at a cost of
  ₹2,086 lakh (₹1,400 per tree including three years of maintenance). Tree
  canopy in Varanasi's built-up areas is critically low in every ward
  analysed (Section 5).

## 4. Heat Hazard Analysis

against a city mean of 43.1 °C. The reference temperature of the city's

The coolest wards are Nariya (41.5 °C, −1.6 °C below the city mean), Sarnath
(41.9 °C, −1.3 °C), Indrapur (42.0 °C, −1.2 °C) and Rajabazar (42.4 °C,
−0.8 °C).

### 4.2 Nighttime heat

Nighttime land surface temperature (MODIS) averages 21.1 °C citywide over the
analysis period. The strongest nighttime hotspots (deviation from the city
nighttime mean) are Lallapura Kala (+1.1 °C), followed by Chetganj,
Lahangpura, Lallapur, Habibpur, Saray Govardhan and Kazipura (+1.0 °C each).

Daytime and nighttime heat do not always coincide. Katuapura is among the
strongest daytime hotspots (+1.7 °C) but shows a modest nighttime deviation
(+0.3 °C), whereas Lallapura Kala shows a moderate daytime deviation
(+0.5 °C) yet is the city's strongest nighttime hotspot (+1.1 °C).
Heat-response planning should therefore treat daytime and nighttime exposure
as distinct hazards.

### 4.3 24-hour heat zones

The 24-Hour Heat Zone score combines daytime and nighttime heat with
population presence and nightlight-derived activity, identifying areas where
people remain exposed around the clock. Eighteen wards are classified High.
The most severe are Lallapur (score 186.1), Nadesar (181.8), Jagatganj
(180.8), Habibpur (171.9) and Lallapura Kala (170.0). These wards combine hot
days, warm nights and sustained human activity, and are the priority
locations for night-shelter provisioning, extended cooling-centre hours and
outdoor-worker protections.

Jolha (17,956 residents, 3.37).

### 5.2 Housing vulnerability

The Dense/Vulnerable Housing score combines building density (GHSL), lack of
vegetation and low nighttime-light infrastructure, each normalised to the
city's own distribution. Seventeen wards are classified High. The most
vulnerable housing stock is found in Jolha (score 77.8), Saraysurjan (76.7),
Saraiya (76.3), Khojawan (71.5), Naibasti (71.4), Sarsauli (70.8) and
Jalalipur (70.2).

### 5.3 Green cover deficit

Tree canopy in Varanasi's built-up areas is critically deficient citywide.
The highest ward canopy figure recorded is 1.14% (Nariya); the majority of
wards record effectively zero canopy within built-up areas — far below the
20% planning target. Ward mean vegetation index (NDVI) ranges from 0.12 to
0.37. The greening urgency indicator is CRITICAL (canopy below 5%) in every
ward analysed.

## 10. Appendix — Data Sources and Methodology

All ward-level figures in this Plan were computed using the CHAITRA
methodology (chaitra.info) over the analysis window 2022-04-01 to 2024-07-31,
restricted to the summer months (April-July).

- **Ward boundaries:** municipal ward shapefile as hosted in CHAITRA
  (Google Earth Engine asset); 89 wards, of which three lack identifiers
  (see data-quality note).
- **Daytime land surface temperature:** Landsat 8/9 Collection 2 Level-2
  thermal data, 30 m resolution, summer median composite, cloud-masked.
- **Nighttime land surface temperature:** MODIS Terra+Aqua (MOD11A1/MYD11A1),
  1 km resolution, 2-year median.
- **Population:** WorldPop 100 m gridded population, 2020.
- **Land cover:** ESA WorldCover v200 (2021), 10 m; tree canopy from Google
  Dynamic World, 10 m.
- **Albedo:** Sentinel-2 broadband albedo (Liang 2001 coefficients), 10 m.
- **Housing vulnerability:** GHSL Built Surface 2020 density, vegetation
  deficit, and VIIRS nightlight dimness, combined by geometric mean and
  normalised to city P5-P95.
- **Composite Heat Risk Index:** IPCC AR6 framework, Risk = (Hazard ×
  Exposure × Vulnerability)^(1/3); Hazard = daytime + nighttime heat hotspot
  scores; Exposure = ln(population+1); Vulnerability = canopy deficit +
  housing density + albedo deficit; classified by city-specific percentiles
  (High ≥ P70, Medium ≥ P50).
- **Cool roof costing:** ₹150-230 per m² (GRIHA 2021 / GEDA 2022-23),
  midpoint ₹190/m².
- **Tree costing:** 150 trees per hectare of canopy deficit, ×1.35 mortality
  buffer, ₹1,400 per tree including 3-year maintenance (CHAITRA layer
  constants).

All classifications (High/Medium/Low) are relative to Varanasi's own
distribution, not fixed thresholds. Every number in Sections 1 and 4-7 was
verified against the source data file by automated check
(`verify_hap_numbers.py`).

## Concluding Caution

This Plan was produced by an automated pipeline with no first-hand knowledge
of Varanasi. Before acting on any recommendation in it, the implementing
authority must: (1) verify conditions on the ground in each ward; (2) confirm
land use, ownership and community significance of every proposed intervention
site — including schools, playgrounds, markets, ghats, religious and heritage
sites, and informal social spaces that the underlying data cannot see;
(3) complete the [BLANK] sections with authoritative local information;
(4) correct the three unidentified ward records in the municipal GIS boundary
file and re-run the analysis for those wards; and (5) treat the figures
herein as screening-level estimates to be superseded by detailed project
surveys. The authors of the data pipeline accept that satellite analysis is
an aid to local judgement, never a replacement for it.
